Chess robot

A SCARA-style arm that moves chess pieces on a physical board, with a camera that reads the board from underneath so a player's hand never blocks the view.

Outcome
Nearing full game capabilites. Confirmed movement of pieces, full working elecrronics. Implimented game algorithm to match opponent level of skill. Working on honing cv pipeline, calibrating board positions & arm movement, as well as fully integrating all parts into full playability.

Choosing the design

Before building, I scored the options for three decisions: the pickup claw, the overall layout, and the arm. The highest total won each time.

Layout. Putting the camera under the board scored 5 on human occlusion risk and 5 on calibration stability, against 3 and 2 for the side robot. It lost points on human accessibility (3, versus 6 for the side robot). I accepted that trade for reliable vision.

Arm. A linear gantry beat SCARA on accuracy (10 vs 8), but scored 3 on impact on the human player against 9 for SCARA. SCARA won overall, 40 to 38.

Claw. The score was close, but waranted testing for viability. After testing it became the confirmed choice.

Early prototyping and testing

The box with the board is mainly made out of wood. The top layer was made through laser cut and engraved wood with a acrylic layer under for the camera to see thorugh. This box houses also houses all electronics. The mechanical arm was origanlly modeled in CAD before assembling. Throughout the process test pieces, and fits were tested to match the board.

The arm: from gears to belts

Along with various strength and tolerance readjustments the major change occured due to the use of gears in the original design. They were too imprecise; the motor position varied by about 2 degrees of motion and there was no identical zero position, which made calibration hard. By switching from gear to belts with proper tensioning systems, the error variabily dropped to nearly zero.

Computer vision

The raspberry pi used the camera under the board tracks the state of each piece on the board. By identifying the colored bases of the pieces, it can track each move played from starting position. There was no need to differently color each piece.

$ python cvtesting.py RED PIECES ON: ['F6', 'E3', 'F3', 'G3', 'A2', 'B2', 'C2', 'D2', 'E2', 'F2', 'G2', 'H2', 'A1', 'B1', 'E1', 'H1'] YELLOW PIECES ON: ['B8', 'C8', 'D8', 'E8', 'F8', 'G8', 'H8', 'A7', 'B7', 'D7', 'E7', 'F7', 'G7', 'H7', 'A6']

The program's output for the same board: 16 red and 15 yellow squares, matching the labels in the image.

Major Complications: The original version misidentified ~10% of pieces. This was due to different lighting conditions changing RGB values heavily. To solve this we changed from RGB to HSV, taking median pixel values over each hole, and taking the most common reading over several frames, which brought errors close to zero.

What I'd change

The entire box made the project compact and reliable, however was quite bulky especially when placed on a table. The height of the board requires standing to play. Preferably, if you could either add more cameras to lower the height of the board, or choose an overhead camera design with piece recognition. Mechanically, the arm has a slight tilt which could be made more rigid with design improvements.

How the robot works

A faster geared up rubber band intake pulls in balls. The transfer stores up to 3 balls inside the robot. A servo gate prevents the intook balls from being prematurly shot before the shooter is up to speed. A hooded shooter controlled by speed allows, the balls to be shot different distances, while creating spin creating consistant trajectories.

Robot code structure

The code is a command-based structure. Each subsytem runs inedpendantly of eachother and are created and used in a larger "RobotContainer." Commands can pull subsystems together to perform more complex tasks. No subsystem waits for another; if one needs data from another a subsytems can publish data and get data supplied from this publishing. This structure allows the system to be robust, overcome runtime errors, and work even if small parts fail.

Aiming while driving

Coded routines to simulataniously aim 2 seperate turrets anywhere on the field with any robot speed. This required, knowing where the robot is based on cameras and odometry, tracking robot speed using drivetrain feedback, characterizing robot's turrets to match real world physics. With all of this information, a iterative newton solve is used to make adjustments to the aiming system to account for thes changing factors.

Vision and localization

2 seperate coproccessors run 4 cameras total to find and track Charuco markers for field localization. This data is filtered and added with a confidence based on standard deviations of vision measurements. A Kalman filter then combines IMU, odometry, and vision data weighted by standard deviations of feedback measurements to provide a robust estimate of the robot's position .

Autonomous routine

I implimented the autonomous routines this season, stiching robot commands into autonomous pathing, testing tolerances, optimizing behaviors, and tuning reactions based on vision and odometry data. This was done without a field test field; required other teams test fields and adjustements during competition.

What I'd change

Many problems with software, camera reliability, and sensors was the lack of error tracking. For examample, one major issue was the coprocessors restarting during system brownouts. This was not figured out until later, to which we added a battery as a buffer. Going forward, larger tracking, such as power monitoring, can make diagnosing such symptoms much easier. Otherwise, the aiming on the move was done through adjusting flywheel speed. If the robot had adjustable hoods, greater accuracy and speed could be established due to requiring less time to adjust.

Self-righting multi-hull vessel (patent application)

As an intern at Neptune Dynamix, I co-invented a passively self-righting multi-hull vessel, now filed as an international patent application.

Company
Neptune Dynamix
My role
Intern and co-inventor, summer of 2025
Status
Provisional patent application (2025), then international (PCT) application (2026)

The invention

A passively self-righting multi-hull vessel: a catamaran-style boat that passively rights itself after capsizing, without motors or controls.

The first testing and prototypes were build using small models. Current custom physics simulation proves viability and adaptability. Current first prototype at full-scale being tested. The idea is that the weights and buoyancy elements on the boat always keep it at an unstable position regardless of angle the boat is currently at. The only stable position is when the boat is upright.

The problem it solves

Most of my robotics work has been in controlled environments, where I could measure what was happening and correct for it with sensors and code. This design pushed me the other way: the water is unpredictable, so instead of sensing and correcting for uncertainty, the boat had to be built to handle it on its own, with no motors or controls to fail.

What I learned

Compared to much of my experience in robotics, most of my situations have been controlled environments. This design specifically challenged me to think about dealing with uncertainty and passively dealing with it rather than manipulating it.
